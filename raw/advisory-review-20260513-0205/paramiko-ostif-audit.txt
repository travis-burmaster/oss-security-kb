2. About OSTIF
The Open Source Technology Improvement Fund (OSTIF) is dedicated to resourcing
and managing security engagements for open source software projects through partnerships
with corporate, government, and non-profit donors. We bridge the gap between resources and
security outcomes, while supporting and championing the open source community whose efforts
underpin our digital landscape.
Over the past ten years, OSTIF has been responsible for the discovery of over 800 vulnerabilities, (121 of those being Critical/High), over 13,000 hours of security work, and millions
of dollars raised for open source security. Maximizing output and security outcomes while
minimizing labor and cost for projects and funders has resulted in partnerships with multibillion dollar companies, top open source foundations, government organizations, and respected
individuals in the space. Most importantly, we’ve helped over 120 projects and counting improve
their security posture.
Our directive is to support and enrich the open source community through providing publicfacing security audits, educational resources, meetups, tooling, and advice. OSTIF’s experience
positions us to be able to share knowledge of auditing with maintainers, developers, foundations,
and the community to further secure our infrastructure in a sustainable manner.
We are a small team working out of Chicago, Illinois. Our website is ostif.org. You can follow
us on social media to keep up to date on audits, conferences, meetups, and opportunities with
OSTIF, or feel free to reach out directly at contactus@ostif.org or our GitHub.

3. Executive Summary
3.1. Context
Paramiko is a pure Python implementation of SSHv2, providing both client and server
functions. It forms the basis for the high-level SSH library Fabric, and it is the most popular
SSH implementation in Python.
Cryptography is a collection that provides access to cryptographic algorithms and methods
for Python programmers. It is a very popular Python/Rust cryptographic library with over
25,000 known dependencies.
This engagement encompasses a comprehensive assessment of the Paramiko library, along
with a detailed examination of the Cryptography library’s interaction with the rust-openssl
bindings, reliable entropy sources, constant-time correctness requirements, code quality, testing
procedures, and the CI/CD pipeline. Recommendations for strengthening these areas will be
provided.

3.2. Objectives
The Paramiko library is designed to be compact, easy to understand, and limited in functionality to minimize the attack surface. Attacks on SSH implementations are well researched and
documented, so OSTIF will direct its efforts towards examining Paramiko’s testing, building,
and CI systems. This will lead to sustainable enhancements in the library’s resilience. It will also
examine how the Paramiko initiative ensures that its dependencies are properly implemented.
Finally, a manual code review will be conducted to verify correctness and that Paramiko is not
vulnerable to known attacks from other SSH implementations.
The Cryptography library boasts a vast array of features and functions, encompassing
numerous use cases. Despite its extensive attack surface, this is made possible through the integration of OpenSSL (via rust-openssl). As such, evaluating the Cryptography library amounts
to examining the proper utilization of rust-openssl’s capabilities, rather than re-examining
cryptographic primitives from the ground up. This review of the library should be initially
triaged to focus on the use cases that affect Paramiko. All remaining time and resources that
are available after the review of the Paramiko use-case should be used in a time-boxed and riskbased approach to evaluate the rest of the Cryptography project.
OpenSSL and rust-openssl themselves are not part of this evaluation, only how they are
invoked by Cryptography and Paramiko. If a researcher finds a potential bug in OpenSSL or
rust-openssl incidentally during this research, they are free to investigate the issue, report it
responsibly and include their findings in the final report. However, this engagement is not a
review of OpenSSL nor rust-openssl.

3.5. Conclusions
Quarkslab has been mandated on behalf of OSTIF to perform the first public security
audit of Paramiko performed by an audit firm. Since critical security features of Paramiko
involve cryptographic primitives, the scope was expanded to PYCA Cryptography and, more
specifically, how Paramiko uses it.
Our work was primarily focused on a detailed, in-depth static analysis, identifying and
developing targeted test enhancements, and dynamic testing where possible. We also addressed
potential security risks in the CI/CD pipeline to ensure a secure and robust deployment process.
During the audit period, we found a few issues, but nothing that raises Paramiko or
Cryptography’s security concerns. We believe that all of them can be easily corrected or
implemented as best practices.

CRITICAL

Allows a total compromise of the assessed system, allowing an attacker to
read or modify the data stored in the system as well as altering its behavior.

HIGH

Allows an attacker to impact significantly one or more components, giving
access to sensitive data or offering the attacker a possibility to pivot and
attack other connected assets.

MEDIUM

Allows an attacker to access some information, or to alter the behavior of the
assessed system with restricted permissions.

LOW

Allows an attacker to access non-sensitive information, or to alter the behavior
of the assessed system and impact a limited number of users.

5.2.2. Likelihood
The vulnerability likelihood is evaluated by taking the following criteria in consideration:
• Access conditions: the vulnerability may require the attacker to have physical access to
the targeted asset or to be present in the same network for instance, or can be directly
exploited from the Internet.
• Required skills: an attacker may need specific skills to exploit the vulnerability.
• Known available exploit: when a vulnerability has been published and an exploit is
available, the probability a non-skilled attacker would find it and use it is pretty high.
The following table summarizes the different level of vulnerability likelihood:
CRITICAL

The vulnerability is easy to exploit even from an unskilled attacker and has
no specific access conditions.

HIGH

The vulnerability is easy to exploit but requires some specific conditions to
be met (specific skills or access).

MEDIUM

The vulnerability is not trivial to discover and exploit, requires very specific
knowledge or specific access (internal network, physical access to an asset).

LOW

The vulnerability is very difficult to discover and exploit, requires highly
specific knowledge or authorized access

6. Discovery
6.1. Projects Information
Paramiko is a pure Python implementation of the SSHv2 protocol, providing both client
and server capabilities. It serves as the foundation for Fabric, a more advanced SSH library
that is recommended for everyday tasks such as running remote shell commands or transferring
files. Using Paramiko directly is best for those requiring advanced or low-level control, or who
wants to create an SSH server (sshd) inside Python.
The scope of this audit is defined as follows:
• Paramiko critical cryptographic functions;
• (PYCA) Cryptography’s OpenSSL Rust Bindings;
• CI/CD CircleCI for Paramiko and Github Actions for (PYCA) Cryptography.

8. Paramiko’s compliance to SSH RFCs
The Secure Shell protocol (SSH) is a widely used protocol for securely sending commands to
a computer over an insecure network. A comprehensive overview can be found in a Cloudflare
blogpost (see here).
The detailed guidelines and requirements for this protocol were established in various RFCs
in 2006. Subsequent errata and updates have been made to ensure its continued security and
compatibility with the latest cryptographic primitives.
The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) maintains a datatracker page for the working
group that developed SSH. There, you will find all the relevant RFCs, including their errata
and updates (see here). The most important RFCs for implementing SSH securely are:
• RFC 4251: Defines the SSH architecture and the main security considerations for each of
its components.
• RFC 4252: Defines the SSH authentication protocol for a client to prove its identity to a
server over an already secure SSH transport layer connection.
• RFC 4253: Defines the SSH transport layer protocol for the establishment of a secure
communication between the client and the server by providing encryption, server authentication, integrity protection, and optional compression over TCP/IP.
• RFC 4254: Defines the SSH connection protocol by enabling multiple logical “channels” (shell sessions, command execution, file transfer, etc.) over a single encrypted
connection established by the transport and authentication layers.

8.1. Compliance and Security considerations
Please note that the naming of the different compliance and security checks comes from the
RFCs directly.

8.1.1. Overall
8.1.1.1. Host Keys
Servers hold host keys, which they use for verification during key exchange. This way, clients
can be sure they are communicating with the correct server. Multiple hosts may have the same
host keys.
Within Paramiko’s host key system, multiple key types (RSA, ECDSA, Ed25519) are
supported, with automatic handling of SHA-2 extensions for RSA keys. The server_key_dict
stores all available keys, but only the negotiated key type is used during the actual SSH session.
On the client side, host key verification is managed separately through the HostKeys class, which
handles known_hosts files.

8.2. Summary of RFC compliance
Generally speaking, Paramiko adheres to the assorted RFCs that establish the SSH protocol.
The primary cause of problems is that Paramiko delegates some functions to the application
level. Indeed, the library employs a philosophy of ‘offer tools, let applications decide policy’,
leaving it to applications and system administrators to implement necessary security measures,
such as debug message management.
While this is not a direct problem, we think that using abstract methods to define key
functions would clearly show system administrators the importance of these functions, and that
their implementation is mandatory, improving overall security.

9. Static analysis
9.1. Cryptographic primitives used in Paramiko
During the static analysis phase, we examined the usage of cryptographic primitives by
Paramiko, particularly calls to standard and third-party libraries such as cryptography, hashlib,
bcrypt, and pynacl. This section details the files that directly make use of these libraries. For
each instance, we report our discoveries or lack thereof.
Deprecation of SHA-1 and MD5
We found uses of the deprecated cryptographic primitives SHA-1 and MD5 throughout
the code base. MD5 is not part of the hash algorithms approved by the NIST (see FIPS
180-4, FIPS 202, and SP 800-185) and practical collision attacks have been published (see,
for example, RFC 6151). While SHA-1 is part of FIPS 180-4, the NIST SP 800-131A Rev.
2 deprecates it, disallowing its use for generating digital signatures, with NIST indicating
that SHA-1 should be phased out by 2030 for all other uses.
Per the RFCs describing the SSH protocol and extensions, some of these uses are
expected to provide full compatibility with existing clients. As such, we noted all uses
of these primitives, analyzing whether their use represents an immediate vulnerability, in
which case we added a finding.

9.4. Conclusion
9.4.1. Paramiko
During the static analysis phase, we assessed Paramiko’s usage of cryptographic primitives
and random number generators. We did not find issues in the way Paramiko uses cryptographic
libraries such as cryptography. However, we found uses of deprecated or obsolete primitives
and parameters, such as 1024-bit Diffie-Hellman groups or RSA signatures with SHA-1. These
choices come from the original set of RFCs, which have since been superseded by new specifications that updated the list of recommended primitives and parameters.
We recommend applying the necessary corrections for all instances noted in this section.
We acknowledge that these changes are incompatible with older servers and clients. However,
in most cases, OpenSSH has already implemented the recommended changes. We noted the
specific version whenever possible.
Regarding random number generation, we did not find issues during this phase when the
values generated of urandom are used directly, nor when they are used to generate integers in
specific ranges. However, for most cases, key generation is not covered, as it is usually done
by OpenSSL through cryptography. Both RNG use and key generation are covered by our
additions to the test suite in Section 10.2.2.
In conclusion, we have some additional recommendations.

Ref.: 25-11-2415-REP

113

Quarkslab SAS

Recommendation: ChaCha20-Poly1305
Add support for ChaCha20-Poly1305 as a cipher. This is the default choice in OpenSSH
since version 6.9 and the primitive is implemented by Cryptography (see Authenticated
encryption).
Recommendation: hybrid PQ key agreement
Add support for the hybrid post-quantum key agreement algorithm mlkem768x25519-sha256.
This is the default choice in OpenSSH since version 10.0 (released in April 2025) and version
10.1 will warn users when a non post-quantum key agreement algorithm is used. However,
Cryptography does not offer ML-KEM yet but integration through OpenSSL 3.5 is planned
(see Issue 12610).
Recommendation: deterministic ECDSA
Paramiko introduced deterministic ECDSA signatures in version 1.14.0 (see PR 299) in order
to prevent issues that arise when generating the random parameter 𝑘 (a poor entropy source
can lead up to the compromise of the private key). However, when replacing PyCrypto with
PYCA’s cryptography in version 2.0.0, Paramiko also re-introduced “randomized” ECDSA
as cryptography did not support deterministic ECDSA. Now that PYCA added deterministic
ECDSA to cryptography in version 45.0.0, we recommend using it.

9.4.2. PYCA Cryptography
Many pieces of code rely on OpenSSL’s default behavior. Setting it up correctly is essential, and this should happen, since PYCA Cryptography begins with the OpenSSL binding
(src/cryptography/hazmat/bindings/openssl/binding.py).
Warning
We have discovered numerous instances in PYCA Cryptography Rust implementation where
errors from OpenSSL are converted to a unwrapor(false) instead of specific Python exceptions. This makes errors from the code indistinguishable and could make an attacker tamper
information.

10.1. Increasing test coverage
As the coverage is not a perfect 100%, we examined whether the functions that use
cryptographic primitives were fully covered. To do so, we used the mapping from Section 9.1 in
conjunction with the coverage information to identify any lines of code that were not covered
in those functions. For each such line, we added specific test cases designed to cover it.

given to the client.
Some situations were easy to address. Typically, any adverse scenario involving incorrect
input that results in an exception was not included in the testing. By doing so, we could
guarantee that the software handled these situations effectively, avoiding undesirable outcomes
such as service interruptions or faulty functioning.
Certain instances were simple to activate, yet they enabled us to execute more code.

both as a Python library and as a command-line interface (CLI). For a given algorithm,
crypto-condor executes the relevant test vectors: a set of predefined inputs are given to the
implementation, and the outputs are compared to the test vector values.

module that exposes functions following the API naming convention can be tested with cryptocondor directly. For example, to test the X25519 key exchange, the Python module must export

10.2.2. Randomness generation
In order to test randomness generation, Quarkslab uses a battery of statistical tests based on
TestU01, a C library for empirical testing of random number generators. This specific battery
is composed of 29 statistical tests that correspond to those used by NIST Statistical Test Suite,
with several sets of parameters. They are available through crypto-condor.
We have identified three categories of randomness generation: direct uses of os.urandom,
functions that generate random numbers using os.urandom, and third-party functions that
require a good random number generator (e.g. a key generation function). As detailed in
Section 9.2, we assume that os.urandom is a strong CSPRNG using a good source of entropy.
In contrast, the functions such as _roll_random() that build upon the output of os.urandom to
generate numbers in specific intervals require a more detailed study. Third-party libraries are
out of the scope of this audit, but the quality of the random values generated by them must be
tested to ensure that no issues exist.
To test these two generators of randomness, we can sample considerable amounts of data
and treat it as the output of a PRNG to test it with TestU01. Particularly, we are looking for
indications that the output of these functions may be biased.

These classes are not part of the public API of Paramiko, but we can still access the

• The key generation with _generate_key_pair();
• The actual exchange with the start_kex() and parse_next() methods.
The key generation function is covered in Section 10.2.2. We added two crypto-condor
harnesses to test the key exchange, one from the client perspective and one from the server.
In either case, the exchange is performed with the start_kex() and parse_next() methods.
The first one calls _generate_key_pair(), which will generate a key pair for the corresponding
elliptic curve. To test the exchange, crypto-condor gives the function the two keys required to
perform the exchange (the private key of one party and the public key of the other). To use
these keys, we mock the key generation to return the ones given by crypto-condor. The output
of the exchange should match the value from the test vector.
Success
Both tests for client and server key exchange pass.

11.4. Constant-timeness
Cryptographic implementations should be constant time to prevent timing side-channel
attacks, where an attacker can infer secret information—such as encryption keys—by measuring
how long operations take to execute. Variations in execution time caused by data-dependent
branches or memory accesses can unintentionally reveal patterns linked to sensitive values. By
ensuring that all operations run in constant time, regardless of input data, implementations can
protect against these subtle but powerful attacks and maintain the confidentiality and integrity
of cryptographic systems.
To test for the use of sensitive material in conditional branches, we used Pysa, the Python
Static Analyzer feature of Pyre, a Python type-checker. This analyzer implements taint
analysis: it tracks values that come from user-defined sources to check if they can reach a
“dangerous location” called sink.
While Pysa generally deals with functions as sinks, it can also use conditional branches as
implicit sinks. This fits our use-case: we identified all sensitive values (static and ephemeral
keys, passwords), marked them as tainted sources, and used Pysa to verify if any of those values
ends up in a conditional branch.
The current approach suffers from two drawbacks:

Ref.: 25-11-2415-REP

128

Quarkslab SAS

• The implicit sinks feature relies only on forward analysis, while explicit sinks also rely on
backward analysis. From our testing, forward analysis misses cases in which the conditional
branch is not in the same module as the tainted source while backward analysis can find them.
• Most keys are not raw byte strings but are opaque attributes of a class (particularly in
cryptography keys, which come from the rust_openssl bindings). This can result in many
false positives, as we can only mark the class as tainted, which is flagged whenever the class
itself or an innocuous method is used in a conditional branch, but the key material is not
exposed.
The following sections indicate the instances found by Pysa: all are false positives. Illustrating the second drawback, these instances are mostly false positives due to the use of the
class itself in a conditional branch (e.g., checking if the object has a particular type). However
one instance is notable: in Section 11.4.3, the result of the key exchange is used in a (constanttime) comparison, and has been flagged because the key attribute of KexCurve25519 is tainted.
This showcases the power of taint analysis for sensitive variable propagation.

operations. Inspired by the cryptofuzz project, DeltAFLy addresses several of its limitations
through a more modular architecture where each harness is packaged as an independent shared
library.
It supports differential fuzzing across multiple languages, including Rust, C, C++, and Java.

capable of detecting secret-dependent control flow. DeltAFLy notably led to the discovery of
vulnerabilities in the HQC post-quantum algorithm implementation within LibOQS.
DeltAFLy operates as a greybox differential fuzzer, combining both coverage feedback and
input/output comparison to efficiently guide its exploration. The results of the performed tests

12.1. Paramiko’s CI
CircleCI is a continuous integration and deployment (CI/CD) platform that automates
software development cycles. Its operation relies on a configuration file (.circleci/config.yml)
defining pipelines executed in ephemeral environments (Docker containers or virtual machines),
automatically triggered by Git-related events. These pipelines consist of jobs (tests, linting,
deployment) orchestrated by workflows, with secrets and environment variables managed
through contexts and project-level settings.
A key aspect involves SSH integration. CircleCI supports storing dedicated SSH keys to clone
private repositories or manage submodules, with secure injection during pipeline execution.
Temporary SSH sessions can also be opened to debug running jobs. Security best practices
recommend using CircleCI-specific SSH keys with limited permissions and validating remote
hosts via the known_hosts mechanism to mitigate man-in-the-middle attacks.
Pull request jobs run inside ephemeral Docker containers without exposed Docker sockets or
elevated privileges, providing appropriate isolation. The residual risk is primarily configurationrelated (improper injection of secrets, overly verbose logs). Recommended measures include
PR/trusted separation, prohibition of secrets in PRs, opt-in debug filtering, and maintaining
non-privileged execution.
The project being open source, it is possible to find the CircleCI pipeline on the following
URL: https://app.circleci.com/pipelines/github/paramiko/paramiko.
However, certain limitations are inherent to CircleCI. For instance, API keys do not support
fine-grained permissions: users either have full access or none at all. This lack of granularity
makes the compromise of an API key particularly critical.

12.2. Fork to Pull Requests
Several attack vectors may nonetheless lead to CI compromise. For instance, it has been
demonstrated that pipelines can be triggered through a pull request originating from a fork.
In such a scenario, an attacker may execute arbitrary runners. However, these runners are not

12.3. Supply Chain Attacks
It is also possible to target Paramiko through its dependencies, in particular the PYCA/
Cryptography library. Paramiko has a single primary maintainer, which reduces the likelihood
of supply-chain abuse originating from its own contributor base. Cryptography, on the other
hand, is maintained by a significantly larger community with more than 300 contributors, which
increases the potential attack surface (see Figure 30).

the scan did not reveal any vulnerable dependencies. Nevertheless, an attacker could infiltrate
the contributor community of Cryptography and inject malicious code that would subsequently
propagate to Paramiko through its direct dependency.
Several high-profile supply-chain compromises in recent years illustrate this threat model.
The XZ Utils backdoor campaign, for example, involved the introduction of malicious code

perimeter. This case demonstrates how a persistent and targeted campaign against a dependency can enable remote code execution or privilege escalation on downstream systems.
Mitigation measures include regular dependency audits, strict peer reviews, multi-person
approval for modifications to build or release tools, minimizing direct dependency exposure
where possible, and monitoring contributors’ activity for signs of sock puppet accounts or longterm infiltration attempts.

def build_graph(root: str, max_depth: int, installed_map: Dict[str, str]) ->
nx.DiGraph:
G = nx.DiGraph()
root_canon = canonicalize_name(root)
# BFS
queue: List[Tuple[str, int]] = [(root_canon, 0)]
seen: Set[str] = set()
while queue:
node, depth = queue.pop(0)
if node in seen:
continue
seen.add(node)
G.add_node(node)
if depth >= max_depth:
continue
# Get dependencies from installed metadata if present
child_names: List[str] = []
# importlib.metadata expects original name, not canonicalized in lookup
# Try best-effort: find any installed dist matching canonical name.

for n, v in installed_map.items():
if canonicalize_name(n) == node:
lookup_name = n
break
raw = get_requires_for_dist(lookup_name or node)
child_names = parse_dependencies(raw)
for dep in child_names:
G.add_edge(node, dep)
queue.append((dep, depth + 1))
return G
